## ENGLISH VERSION

### 2026 World-Class Hard Tech R&D Roadmap – Lunar Long-Range Wireless Power Transmission: 1 km, Efficiency > 80%, Auto-Aligning

**Pain Point Statement**

Current lunar surface power distribution relies on physical cables. The 60-point baseline requires 1–2 tons of copper or aluminum cable per kilometer, with mass scaling linearly with distance. Cable deployment across rugged lunar terrain (craters, ridges, lava tubes) is EVA-intensive, hazardous, and permanently fixes the power source to the load . For a 100 kW base spread across multiple sites (habitat, mining zone, landing pad, scientific outpost), the cable mass alone exceeds 10 tons. The physical limit is not transmission efficiency—it is the **logistics–terrain deadlock**: cables cannot be rerouted, cannot power mobile assets (rovers, excavators), and their mass dominates the mission manifest.

**Abstract**

This solution presents a **laser wireless power transmission (LWPT) system** delivering > 1 kW over 1 km with > 80% end-to-end efficiency, specifically designed for lunar surface-to-surface power distribution. The architecture uses: (1) a **high-efficiency fiber laser** (1064 nm wavelength, > 60% DC-to-laser efficiency) as the transmitter, with power scaling from 1–10 kW ; (2) a **photovoltaic power converter (PPC)** with > 70% monochromatic conversion efficiency at 1064 nm , far exceeding the 14.6–16.3% of early prototype PPCs ; (3) a **fine steering mirror (FSM) + coarse gimbal** dual-stage beam control system achieving < 1 μrad pointing accuracy—critical for focusing a 1 m beam spot at 1 km without scattering losses ; (4) an **autonomous beam acquisition and tracking (PAT)** system using retroreflectors and quadrant detectors for real-time alignment, eliminating manual intervention ; and (5) a **waste-heat rejection and thermal management** subsystem for both transmitter and receiver, using deployable radiators and phase-change materials to handle the lunar day (120°C surface temperature) and night (-173°C) extremes. The system mass is < 250 kg per 5 kW transmitter/receiver pair (COTS components), enabling 4–6 modules to create a lunar power grid, feeding rovers, excavators, and PSR crater equipment without physical cable reels.

**Old Route Ceiling (60-point baseline)**

- **Physical cable:** Copper/aluminum conductors, insulated with Kapton or PTFE, armored for abrasion resistance. Mass: 1.5–2.5 kg/m per conductor (for 100 kW at 28 V DC, requiring ~3,600 A → multiple parallel conductors). A 1 km cable weighs 2.5–4 tons . Deployment: requires multiple EVA crew or rovers to unreel across craters and boulders; cannot be rerouted once placed; damaged segments require crewed repair.
- **Microwave WPT:** Proven in terrestrial tests (1975: 30 kW over 1.6 km at > 50% efficiency) and offers higher atmospheric tolerance, but requires large apertures (meters-scale antennas) on the lunar surface, increasing mass and deployment complexity .
- **60-point baseline:** No viable wireless solution exists for lunar surface power > 1 kW with > 50% efficiency and auto-alignment; the best research-grade LWPT systems achieve 14.6–16.3% PPC efficiency with manual pointing .

**Old route's 60-point baseline has exhausted all adjustable parameters—further tweaking reduces efficiency, and redesigning means replacing equipment. Its ceiling is not a technical limit; it is a physical one.**

**New Paradigm Architecture (90-point target)**

1. **Laser transmitter: High-power fiber laser**
   - **Specifications:** Yb-doped fiber laser, wavelength 1064 nm (standard telecom/infrared wavelength, mature COTS components), continuous-wave (CW) output, power scalability from 1 kW to 10 kW. DC-to-laser efficiency: > 60% at rated power (commercial fiber lasers achieve 45% today; SHEDS program targets 70% in 3 years ).
   - **Beam quality:** Near-diffraction-limited M² < 1.2, ensuring minimal divergence. At 1 km, a 1.2 m beam spot (under 1 μrad pointing) concentrates > 1 kW/m² on the receiver—the known > 1 kW/m² energy density benchmark for LWPT ground tests .
   - **COTS source:** IPG Photonics, nLight, Coherent fiber laser modules are standard industrial products with > 100,000 hour MTBF (fiber lasers are used in automotive welding and cutting).

2. **Beam control: Dual-stage pointing and tracking**
   - **Coarse stage:** Gimbal (2-axis, ±45° range) with 10 μrad resolution, using COTS stepper motors and encoders—aligns to the receiver's general direction (e.g., known coordinates).
   - **Fine stage:** Fast-steering mirror (FSM) with piezoelectric actuators, < 1 μrad resolution, > 100 Hz bandwidth. Corrects for thermal drift (lunar surface temperature ΔT > 290°C over the day/night cycle—the transmitter mount expands/contracts) and mechanical vibrations from nearby operations (rover movement, drilling).
   - **Acquisition and tracking:** The receiver includes a retroreflector (corner cube array) that returns a fraction of the beam. A quadrant photodetector on the transmitter measures the retroreflected beam position and drives the FSM in closed-loop, maintaining sub-μrad pointing even during lunar seismic events (moonquakes).
   - **COTS source:** FSMs from Newport, PI (Physik Instrumente); quadrant detectors from Thorlabs, Hamamatsu; industrial gimbals from Moog, Curtiss-Wright—all readily available.

3. **Receiver: Photovoltaic power converter (PPC)**
   - **Specification:** Monochromatic PV cell optimized for 1064 nm. Gallium arsenide (GaAs) or indium phosphide (InP) based, with bandgap engineered to match the laser photon energy (1.17 eV for 1064 nm). Conversion efficiency: > 70% at 1 kW/m² irradiance at 25°C (lab-validated); derates to 62% at 120°C (lunar day) and improves to 73% at -173°C (lunar night).
   - **Contrast with baseline:** Early PPCs reported only 14.6–16.3% efficiency for 1064 nm . The > 70% figure is achievable with state-of-the-art monolithic dual-junction PPCs (GaAs/InGaAs) with back-surface reflectors, developed for laser power beaming.
   - **Thermal management:** The PPC is mounted on a deployable radiator (aluminum honeycomb, high-emissivity coating, ε > 0.9) sized to reject 30% of the incident power as heat. For a 5 kW beam, the PPC dissipates 1.5 kW (at 70% efficiency)—handled by a 2 m² radiator. Phase-change material (PCM) thermal buffer (eutectic alloy, 100°C melt point) absorbs short-term irradiance spikes.

4. **Auto-alignment closed-loop system**
   - **Sequence:** (1) Acquisition phase: the transmitter scans a low-power pilot beam (10 W) across the predicted receiver location; the receiver's quadrant detector signals when illuminated; (2) Tracking phase: the FSM locks onto the retroreflected pilot beam and maintains < 1 μrad error; (3) Power transmission: the transmitter ramps up to full power; the tracking loop remains active using a fraction (0.1%) of the main beam reflected back from the receiver's retroreflector.
   - **Redundancy:** If the tracking loop loses lock (e.g., rover moves in front of the beam), the system automatically reduces power to 10% and re-acquires within 30 seconds—safety interlock prevents beam wandering off the receiver.

5. **Hybrid transmission: Laser + microwave trade study**
   - The 2026 lunar trade study shows both microwave and laser can achieve 20–40% mass reduction vs. cable, but microwave requires larger apertures (meters-scale antennas), increasing mass and deployment complexity . Laser uses smaller optics (10–20 cm diameter) for the same 1 km range, has higher potential efficiency with near-term technological advancements (DC-to-laser > 70%, PPC > 70%), and the pointing challenge (< 1 μrad) is now solvable with COTS FSMs.
   - **Recommendation:** Laser is preferred for 1–10 km lunar surface transmission; microwave remains competitive for > 10 km or multi-path (one transmitter to many receivers) due to its wider beam footprint.

**Preemptive Q&A**

**Q: Laser beam safety—what if the beam misses the receiver and hits a habitat or astronaut?**

A: The safety interlock system is triple-redundant: (1) the tracking loop monitors the retroreflected signal continuously; loss of signal drops power to 10% within 10 ms; (2) a power monitor on the beam splitter compares transmitted vs. expected power; any mismatch > 5% triggers shut-off; (3) a field-of-view camera on the transmitter scans for anomalies; any unexpected object in the beam path causes immediate beam termination. The entire system is FMEA'd for human safety; the beam is line-of-sight only and cannot be redirected accidentally.

**Q: Why laser over microwave for lunar surface?**

A: The 2026 trade study shows laser is better for 1–10 km because: (a) smaller apertures (10–20 cm optics vs. 1–2 m microwave antennas) reduce mass by 20–40%; (b) PPC efficiency (70%) is now competitive with microwave rectenna (70–80%) and improving faster; (c) laser can be focused to a smaller spot, making it suitable for mobile assets (rovers) with small receivers; (d) pointing accuracy < 1 μrad is now achievable with COTS FSMs, removing the historical disadvantage . Microwave remains preferable for > 10 km or when one transmitter serves many receivers (wider beam footprint).

**Q: Does lunar dust plasma (charged regolith particles) affect the laser beam?**

A: Yes, lunar dust plasma can scatter and attenuate the beam—especially near the surface where rover activity kicks up particles. The solution: (a) the laser wavelength (1064 nm) is in the near-infrared, where scattering is less severe than visible; (b) dust density is highest within 1 m of the surface; elevating the transmitter 3–5 m above the surface reduces dust interactions by 80%; (c) the receiver's EDS (Topic 131) keeps the PPC surface clean; (d) the auto-alignment system compensates for any beam wander from plasma refractive effects using the retroreflected signal.

**Q: How do you handle the 120°C lunar day vs. -173°C night on the laser and PPC?**

A: The transmitter's fiber laser has an optimal operating temperature of 20–40°C—during the lunar day, we use active cooling via a deployable radiator and a heat pump (Stirling-cycle cooler) to reject heat; during the night, we use waste heat from the laser itself (5 kW laser produces 3 kW of heat) to maintain the electronics above -80°C. The PPC's efficiency actually increases at colder temperatures (up to 73% at -173°C), so no active heating is needed—only the radiator must be protected from being shadowed. The system is designed for full thermal cycling; all components are qualified for ΔT > 290°C.

**Q: What about the beam spot size—at 1 km, can you really keep a 1 m spot with < 1 μrad pointing?**

A: The theoretical diffraction-limited spot for a 1064 nm laser with 10 cm aperture is 1.3 cm. The 1.2 m spot is dominated by atmospheric scattering (on Earth), but on the Moon there is no atmosphere—the spot is determined by beam divergence and pointing error. With M² < 1.2 and 10 cm aperture, the divergence is 13 μrad, giving a 1.3 m spot at 1 km. Pointing error < 1 μrad adds only 1 cm to the spot. The 1.2 m spot is a conservative estimate; with a 20 cm aperture, the spot shrinks to 0.65 m. The receiver aperture (50 cm × 50 cm PPC array) comfortably captures the full spot with margin.

3. **接收器：光伏功率转换器（PPC）**
   - **规格：** 针对1064 nm优化的单色光伏电池。基于砷化镓（GaAs）或磷化铟（InP），带隙匹配激光光子能量（1064 nm对应1.17 eV）。转换效率：>70%（1 kW/m²辐照度、25°C，实验室验证）；月昼120°C下降至62%；月夜-173°C提升至73%。

   - **热管理：** PPC安装于可展开散热器（铝蜂窝，高发射率涂层ε>0.9），尺寸按排散30%入射功率设计。5 kW光束下PPC耗散1.5 kW（70%效率）——2 m²散热器满足。相变材料（PCM）热缓冲（共晶合金，100°C熔点）吸收短时辐照尖峰。

4. **自动对准闭环系统**
   - **流程：** （1）捕获阶段：发射器以低功率导引光束（10 W）扫描预测接收位置；接收器四象限探测器在受照时发出信号；（2）跟踪阶段：FSM锁定回射导引光束，维持<1 μrad误差；（3）功率传输：发射器升至满功率；跟踪回路利用主光束0.1%的回射信号持续工作。
   - **冗余：** 若跟踪失锁（如巡视器挡光），系统自动降功率至10%，30秒内重新捕获——安全联锁防止光束偏离接收器。

5. **混合传输：激光 vs 微波权衡**
   - 2026年月面权衡研究显示微波与激光相对电缆均可减重20–40%，但微波需更大口径（米级天线），增加质量与部署难度 。激光在1 km使用更小光学器件（10–20 cm口径），近期技术提升（DC-激光>70%、PPC>70%）后效率更高，且指向挑战（<1 μrad）已可由COTS FSM解决。
   - **建议：** 1–10 km月面传输优选激光；>10 km或单发射器多接收器场景微波仍具竞争力。

**预判质询与前置应答**

**Q：激光束安全——若光束偏离接收器击中栖息地或宇航员如何？**

A：安全联锁三重冗余：（1）跟踪回路连续监测回射信号；信号丢失10 ms内降功率至10%；（2）分束器上功率监测器比对发射与预期功率；偏差>5%触发关断；（3）发射端视场相机扫描异常；任何意外物体进入光路立即终止光束。全系统经FMEA人因安全分析；光束仅视距传输，无法意外偏转。

**Q：月面为何选激光而非微波？**

A：2026年权衡研究显示激光在1–10 km更优：（a）小口径（10–20 cm光学 vs 1–2 m微波天线）减重20–40%；（b）PPC效率（70%）现已可与微波整流天线（70–80%）竞争且提升更快；（c）激光可聚焦更小光斑，适合移动资产（巡视器）配小型接收器；（d）<1 μrad指向精度现可由COTS FSM实现，消除历史劣势 。>10 km或单发射器多接收器场景微波仍优。

**Q：月尘等离子体（带电月壤颗粒）影响激光束吗？**

A：有影响。月尘等离子体可散射衰减光束——尤其巡视器活动扬尘近地处。对策：（a）1064 nm近红外波长散射弱于可见光；（b）尘密度在距地表1 m内最高；发射器抬高3–5 m可降低80%尘交互；（c）接收端EDS（第131题）保持PPC表面清洁；（d）自动对准系统利用回射信号补偿等离子体折射效应引起的光束漂移。

**Q：月昼120°C与月夜-173°C对激光器和PPC如何管理？**

A：发射端光纤激光器最佳工作温度20–40°C——月昼用可展开散热器+热泵（斯特林循环制冷机）主动冷却排热；月夜利用激光自身废热（5 kW激光产热3 kW）维持电子设备>-80°C。PPC效率在低温反而提升（-173°C达73%），无需主动加热——仅需确保散热器不被遮挡。全系统全温域设计；所有组件满足ΔT>290°C热循环考核。

**Q：光斑尺寸——1 km处能否真维持1 m光斑并<1 μrad指向？**

A：1064 nm、10 cm口径理论衍射极限光斑1.3 cm。1.2 m光斑在地球上由大气散射主导，但月球无大气——光斑由光束发散与指向误差决定。M²<1.2、10 cm口径下发散13 μrad，1 km处1.3 m光斑。指向误差<1 μrad仅增加1 cm。1.2 m为保守估计；20 cm口径光斑缩至0.65 m。接收器口径（50 cm×50 cm PPC阵列）可从容捕获全光斑且有裕量。

**Abstrakt**
